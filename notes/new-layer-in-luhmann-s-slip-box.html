New layer in luhmann’s slip box

From storing cards to letting them talk to each other

note-taking
writing
essay
Author

Muhammad Azani Hasibuan

Published

July 10, 2022

Modified

October 1, 2026

Abstract

Writing ideas on separate cards is an old scholarly practice. What Niklas Luhmann added was not the card but the connection: a numbering scheme and a habit of cross-referencing that turned a filing cabinet into a network of ideas. This essay contrasts the classic card-file method with Luhmann’s slip box and explains why the added layer of links matters for thinking and writing.

Keywords

note-taking, Zettelkasten, Luhmann, knowledge management

1 Introduction

Card note-taking systems are not a new approach to note-taking from reading. Long before digital note apps, scholars copied quotations, facts and ideas onto index cards, one idea per card, and filed them in boxes. Guides such as Goutor’s (1980) The Card-File System of Note-Taking taught students to do exactly this when preparing essays and theses.

Niklas Luhmann, the German sociologist, used cards too. Yet his Zettelkasten, or slip box, is remembered not because it contained cards but because of what he did between the cards. He added a new layer to the old method: a way for cards to communicate with each other (Luhmann 1981).

2 The classic card-file method

The card-file method rests on two strengths. The first is atomicity: each card holds a single idea, quotation or fact, so it can be understood on its own. The second is flexibility: because the cards are loose, they can be shuffled, grouped and regrouped. When writing, the scholar pulls out every card filed under a theme, spreads them on a desk, and arranges them into the order of an argument.

The weakness lies in how the cards are stored. In the classic system each card receives a subject heading or code, and it lives in the drawer for that subject (Figure 1). A card about “feedback” sits under Assessment; a card about “motivation” sits under Psychology. The cards are atomic and flexible, but they are also isolated. Any connection between them exists only in the writer’s head, and only at the moment of writing.

3 Luhmann’s new layer: connection

Luhmann kept some 90,000 cards over four decades, and he credited the slip box as a partner in producing dozens of books and hundreds of articles (Schmidt 2016). Two design choices made this possible.

First, he gave every card a fixed, branching number instead of a subject code. A new card was placed directly behind the card it most closely continued. If card 21/3 already had a successor 21/4, a new thought on 21/3 became 21/3a, and a comment on that became 21/3a1. The box could grow inward at any point, forever, without reorganisation. Position no longer meant “this belongs to topic X”; it meant “this continues that thought”.

Second, and more importantly, he linked cards. Whenever he wrote a card, he noted the numbers of other related cards, however distant in the box. A card on feedback could point to one on motivation and another on dashboards, even though they had been written years apart. A small keyword index then provided a handful of entry points into these chains, rather than a complete catalogue (Figure 2).

4 Why the links matter

Links change what the box can do. In a silo system, you retrieve what you filed; you get back exactly what you expected. In a linked system, following a reference from one card leads to another you had forgotten, and from there to a third. Luhmann described the box as a communication partner because it could surprise him: it offered combinations of ideas that he had not planned (Luhmann 1981).

The links also move thinking earlier in the process. Deciding where a new card belongs and which cards it should point to forces you to ask how the idea relates to what you already know. That act is itself a form of reflection. Writing, then, becomes less about staring at a blank page and more about following and assembling chains of thought that already exist (Ahrens 2017).

Figure 3 summarises the difference as a workflow: the classic method ends with filing, while Luhmann’s method adds a step of connecting, and that step feeds directly into writing.

5 Conclusion

The card, the drawer and the principle of one idea per note were already well established when Luhmann began. His contribution was a layer of connection on top of them. Instead of storing cards in silos or classifying them by code or label, he let each card refer to others and allowed the network, not the category, to carry meaning. For anyone taking notes today, whether on paper or in a digital tool, the lesson is the same: the value of a note depends less on where you file it than on what you connect it to.
