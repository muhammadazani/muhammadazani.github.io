Academic Policy Framework for Ethical AI Use in Higher Eudcation: Case in Telkom University

Author

Muhammad Azani Hasibuan

Introduction and Rationale

Telkom University recognizes the transformative impact of generative AI tools (e.g. OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini) on higher education. These tools can enhance learning and research by serving as collaborative assistants for idea generation, tutoring, and problem-solving. In fact, Telkom University has already piloted integrating large language models like ChatGPT into several courses to improve teaching and learning quality . However, alongside these opportunities come challenges to academic integrity. Unethical use of AI – such as having a chatbot do one’s assignments – undermines critical thinking and violates the principles of original scholarship . Indonesia’s higher education authorities have stressed the need for policies ensuring students don’t take unethical “shortcuts” with AI, emphasizing that AI should sharpen (not replace) students’ critical reasoning .

This document establishes a comprehensive academic policy framework that encourages the ethical use of AI by students and faculty while clearly defining misconduct in the AI era. It aligns with Indonesia’s national education standards on academic integrity and draws on global best practices in handling AI in academia. The goal is to foster an innovative academic environment where AI serves as a valuable tool without compromising honesty, originality, or learning outcomes.

Guiding Principles: AI as a Collaborative Tool, Not a Substitute

1. Augmentation over Replacement: Telkom University encourages students and faculty to use AI tools to complement their learning and research, rather than replace their own effort and intellect. AI can be a powerful partner for brainstorming, refining writing, translating languages, or exploring code solutions. However, reliance on AI must not come at the cost of original thought or skill development. Students should leverage AI to enhance understanding and creativity, not to avoid learning . For example, it is acceptable to have ChatGPT help generate ideas or outline an essay, but the student is expected to write the essay in their own words and critically verify any AI-suggested content.

2. Responsibility and Verification: Any use of AI should be approached with academic responsibility. Users (students or faculty) remain accountable for the content they produce, including portions developed with AI assistance . AI outputs may be incorrect, biased, or incomplete. Therefore, students and staff must verify AI-generated information and claims against credible sources before using it in academic work. In practice, this means double-checking facts, recalculating important results, and ensuring that references provided by an AI are real and accurately applied. Faculty are similarly expected to vet any AI-generated teaching materials or research content for accuracy and proper attribution.

3. Transparency and Attribution: To maintain integrity, the use of AI in any academic work must be transparent. When generative AI has contributed to an assignment, paper, or project, students should disclose that fact and properly attribute the AI’s role (e.g. in a footnote or methodology section), as they would for any source. Failing to acknowledge AI assistance when substantial text, code, or analysis comes from it is considered misrepresentation of authorship. Some global academic standards equate uncredited AI-generated content to plagiarism . Telkom University likewise will require that any AI-generated passages, translations, or solutions explicitly indicated (for instance, prefacing such content with a note, or including the AI prompt and response in an appendix when permitted in an assignment). This transparency allows instructors to evaluate the student’s own contribution and ensures credit is given where due – whether to a human author or an algorithm.

4. Academic Freedom with Instructor Guidance: Telkom University supports responsible experimentation with AI in teaching and learning. Instructors have the academic freedom to incorporate AI-based assignments or allow AI tools in their classes as they see fit to meet learning objectives. In those cases, faculty will provide clear guidelines to students on how AI may or may not be used. Conversely, if an instructor deems AI use inappropriate for a certain task (e.g. a critical thinking essay or a closed-book exam), students must abide by those restrictions. This principle aligns with global best practices: for example, Harvard University’s guidance on AI emphasizes that faculty should be clear about permitted uses (if any) of generative AI in coursework, and students should always seek clarification when in doubt . In summary, AI use in academics is a privilege that must be guided by instructors’ rules and the overarching values of integrity.

Defining Academic Dishonesty in the AI Era

In the context of AI tools, Telkom University defines several forms of academic dishonesty and misconduct, all of which are strictly prohibited:

  • Plagiarism of AI-Generated Content: Presenting text, code, images, or ideas generated by AI as one’s own original work without attribution. This includes copying AI-written essays or solutions and submitting them unchanged, or lightly edited, under the student’s name. As an example, using work created by ChatGPT and failing to cite it is explicitly identified as plagiarism at peer institutions , and Telkom University upholds the same standard. Any AI-generated material used in a submission must be properly credited – for instance, citing the tool and the date of use, per citation guidelines (APA, MLA, etc., have formats for AI content).

  • Unauthorized Assistance/Cheating with AI: Using AI tools in ways that are forbidden by an instructor or assessment rules. This includes employing AI to obtain answers or complete tasks on exams, quizzes, or assignments meant to be done without outside help. For example, if an exam or homework is supposed to be completed individually with no resources, querying an AI for answers is equivalent to using unapproved notes or getting a person to help, and thus constitutes cheating. Even in take-home assignments, if the instructor has not explicitly allowed AI assistance, using it secretly breaches academic honesty. Telkom University considers any AI use that violates an assignment’s instructions to be academic misconduct, just as using hidden notes or collaborating illicitly with a classmate would be.

  • Bypassing Intellectual Effort (“Critical Thinking Evasion”): Relying on AI to do the substantive intellectual work in assignments intended to build students’ own skills. This form of dishonesty is more subtle but important in the academic AI policy. For instance, if a student is assigned to write a reflective essay or solve a complex problem to demonstrate learning, simply prompting an AI to produce the essay or solution short-circuits the learning process. Such over-reliance on AI to avoid challenging intellectual work is against the ethos of education and is considered dishonest. Students must not use AI to circumvent assignments designed to develop their analytical or creative abilities. In short, you cannot have an AI “think for you” when your critical thinking is what is being evaluated.

  • Fabrication and Falsification: AI tools can produce plausible-sounding yet incorrect or made-up information (including false citations or invented data). Knowingly using AI-generated falsehoods in academic work – for example, submitting AI-invented references or data as if they were real – is a serious academic offense. This is akin to fabricating a bibliography or falsifying experimental results. Students are expected to vet the output of AI; claiming ignorance that “the AI made it up” will not excuse the responsibility for ensuring accuracy. Any use of AI that results in inserting unverified, incorrect information or fake sources into academic work may be treated as dishonest conduct unless promptly corrected by the student upon discovery.

  • Collusion with AI without Acknowledgement: While “collusion” typically means unauthorized collaboration between people, here we extend the concept to AI. If students collectively or individually use AI assistance on work that is meant to be solely their own effort, it’s inappropriate. For example, if two students both rely on the same AI-generated answer and submit it, that’s equivalent to collusion via a third-party (the AI). Even a single student using AI could be seen as “colluding” with a non-human agent in a way not allowed. Thus, undeclared collaboration with an AI system falls under academic dishonesty when it results in work that is not the student’s independent effort.

Telkom University’s stance is that traditional academic integrity rules fully apply to work involving AI. The fundamental rule remains: the work you submit must be your own, unless otherwise credited or authorized. Using AI is not a loophole to avoid plagiarism or cheating rules. In fact, the university explicitly includes improper AI use as a violation of academic integrity policy, consistent with updates at other universities (e.g., unacknowledged AI-written text is treated as plagiarism) . Any grey areas or new scenarios will be evaluated in the spirit of these definitions – for example, if a new AI tool emerges, using it in a manner that deceives or misrepresents will likewise be considered misconduct.

Guidelines for Acceptable AI Use in Coursework, Research, and Exams

To help students and staff harness AI productively and ethically, Telkom University outlines the following guidelines on what constitutes acceptable use of AI in academic contexts:

  • Permissible Academic Uses (with Attribution): Students may use AI tools for support in learning – such as brainstorming topics, generating ideas or examples, getting feedback on writing style, checking grammar, translating text for comprehension, or debugging code – provided they follow any course-specific rules and give proper credit. For instance, a student writing a paper can ask ChatGPT to suggest some arguments or critique a draft; a computer science student might use an AI assistant to identify an error in their code. These uses are acceptable as long as the student remains the chief architect of their work. If any AI-generated text or analysis is directly incorporated, it should be marked and cited (e.g., “Generated with the assistance of [Tool Name]”). The student should always be able to explain and take ownership of the work’s substance. Acceptable use never means a student can submit work they don’t understand or couldn’t reproduce without the AI.

  • Instructor-Authorized AI Assistance: Different courses will have different pedagogical approaches to AI. Some instructors may explicitly allow or even encourage AI for certain assignments or projects. In such cases, the instructor will outline what is permitted – for example, an assignment might permit using AI to generate an initial draft or to conduct exploratory research. When AI use is part of an assignment by design, students should follow the instructor’s guidelines on how to document their use. For instance, an assignment might require appending the AI prompts and responses in an appendix , or writing a reflection on how the AI output was utilized and improved upon. Adhering to these directions will ensure the use is within acceptable bounds. If an instructor does not mention AI at all in an assignment, students should err on the side of caution and assume AI is not allowed, or proactively ask the instructor for clarification. Communication is key – when in doubt, always seek permission before using AI on graded work.

  • Use in Research and Thesis Writing: Faculty and graduate students may use AI tools to assist in research-related tasks – for example, literature searches, summarizing large texts, organizing data, or even translating and editing drafts. However, the same principles of honesty and verification apply. If AI is used to generate any content that appears in a publication or thesis, it should be disclosed in a footnote or acknowledgments section (as per academic publication standards emerging globally). Researchers must be vigilant that AI-generated text does not inadvertently contain plagiarized phrases or factual errors, and they are responsible for the integrity of their work . Under no circumstance should a student or researcher have an AI write an entire research paper or thesis chapter that they then present as their own work – that would violate academic integrity and could lead to severe consequences (including thesis invalidation). Acceptable use in research is generally limited to assistance in preliminary drafting and editing, data analysis support, or language help, with the human researcher always curating and controlling the final output.

  • AI in Exams and Quizzes: The default rule is that AI tools are forbidden in examinations (including take-home exams) unless an instructor explicitly permits a specific tool for a specific purpose. Exams are meant to evaluate a student’s own knowledge under controlled conditions. Therefore, using AI (or any external aid) during an exam is considered cheating. The policy extends to online testing environments: seeking answers from ChatGPT or a similar model during an exam session is equivalent to consulting an unauthorized source. Instructors who wish to incorporate AI into assessments (for instance, an open-ended take-home exam where using any resource is allowed) will communicate that clearly. Otherwise, students should assume no AI use is allowed when completing exams or quizzes. After exams, students should also refrain from posting the exam questions into public AI platforms (to respect exam confidentiality and prevent sharing of assessment content).

  • Data Privacy and Ethical Constraints: Even when AI use is permitted academically, users must uphold data privacy and ethics. It is unacceptable to input confidential or sensitive university data (such as unpublished research, proprietary information, or personal student/faculty records) into external AI tools that are cloud-based and not under Telkom University’s control. This guideline parallels existing data protection rules – for example, the University of Texas advises against feeding any confidential information into free AI tools . Additionally, AI should not be used to do anything unethical or illegal, such as generating harmful code, deepfakes of individuals, or discriminatory content, especially not in the name of academic work. All usage must accord with the university’s code of ethics and Indonesia’s laws (e.g., no AI use for academic fraud, harassment, or intellectual property infringement).

By following these acceptable use guidelines, students and faculty can benefit from AI tools in a responsible manner. The overarching theme is: use AI to learn, not to cheat. If used correctly, AI can indeed help students think more critically and creatively – for example, by offering alternative perspectives on a problem that the student can then evaluate. Telkom University encourages such positive uses, in line with the idea that AI tools, when used wisely, can even improve problem-solving and critical thinking skills rather than diminish them . We want our graduates to be adept at using modern tools, but also deeply grounded in ethical practices and independent thinking.

Prohibited AI Uses and Misconduct Scenarios

To remove any ambiguity, the policy specifies unacceptable uses of AI in academic work. These actions will be treated as academic misconduct subject to discipline:

  • Complete Outsourcing of Work to AI: Submitting AI-generated work as if it were one’s own, in full or large part, is strictly prohibited. For example, if a student prompts an AI to write a full essay or solve all problems on an assignment and then turns it in with minimal or no edits, this is an offense. Even if the student proofreads or slightly rephrases the AI output, it doesn’t change the fact that the intellectual work was outsourced. This behavior bypasses the learning process and is equivalent to contract cheating or buying an essay – only the “ghostwriter” in this case is an AI. University policy forbids this, and if detected, the student will face the same penalties as traditional plagiarism or cheating. In short, you cannot delegate your assignments to AI and expect to receive credit.

  • Using AI when Explicitly Forbidden: If an instructor or exam rule bans the use of AI or any external assistance, then using it is an act of cheating. For instance, some programming courses might ban AI code assistants on individual projects to ensure students learn to code themselves. If a student violates that rule by secretly using an AI coding assistant (like GitHub Copilot or ChatGPT) to produce their program, they have committed academic dishonesty. The same goes for written assignments – if the syllabus or professor states that all writing must be original and AI is not allowed, then employing it anyway is a deliberate violation. We emphasize that “unauthorized AI use” is on par with using any unauthorized resource. The absence of a specific mention does not imply permission; students should assume AI is disallowed unless guidelines say otherwise.

  • Failure to Disclose AI Contributions: Even in cases where AI assistance is allowed, failing to disclose that help is unacceptable. For example, if a professor permits using AI to generate ideas for a design project, but a student ends up using an AI-generated design or substantial text in the final submission, the student must acknowledge it. Not doing so is considered a form of plagiarism by omission. The university considers it the student’s duty to be transparent about AI’s role. Hiding AI use suggests the intent to claim credit for work not actually one’s own. Many universities worldwide have updated their honor codes to reflect this; for instance, Carnegie Mellon University notes that passing off AI-generated content as one’s own is a violation of academic integrity . Telkom University holds the same view. Thus, if a student used ChatGPT to compose a paragraph or solve a math proof, they must cite or indicate it – or else it is an offense.

  • AI-Assisted Plagiarism of Sources: A nuanced but important point: if a student uses AI to paraphrase or summarize someone else’s work and then submits it without citation, that is still plagiarism. Some might mistakenly think that if an AI rewrites text, it’s “original” – it is not. Using AI to conceal the extent of copied material (for example, taking a published article, asking AI to paraphrase it, and then submitting as your answer) is academic fraud. The source must be credited, regardless of whether an AI altered the wording. The policy thus prohibits using AI as a tool to evade plagiarism detection or source citation. Any ideas or data from others, whether obtained directly or via AI, must be properly cited to avoid plagiarism.

  • Improper Use of AI in Exams: As stated, using AI during exams is banned. This includes both feeding exam questions into an AI to get answers, and using AI-generated notes or solutions pre-prepared for a closed-book exam. If a student is caught with AI-generated answer material in an exam context, it will be treated as cheating. Additionally, students should not use AI to generate personalized notes or translations during an exam unless it’s explicitly part of an accommodation that the instructor knows about. In summary, any involvement of AI in an exam setting (unless the exam is an open-resource format that allows it) is an academic integrity violation.

  • Breaches of Confidentiality or Privacy with AI: It is also unacceptable to use AI in ways that compromise ethical or legal obligations. For example, uploading a classmate’s essay or unpublished lab data into an AI service (to have the AI analyze or rewrite it) without permission violates privacy and possibly intellectual property rights. Likewise, using AI to generate content that harasses or discriminates against others in the university would violate our code of conduct. Such misuse falls outside the scope of acceptable academic use and will be handled accordingly under misconduct or even disciplinary codes beyond academics.

Telkom University will enforce these prohibitions firmly. They align with what other institutions globally are doing to maintain academic integrity in the age of AI. For instance, the University of Texas at Austin explicitly warns that AI tools should not be used to produce any academic work that instructors have not authorized . The spirit is clear: AI is not an excuse to violate academic honesty standards. Students who engage in the above unacceptable behaviors will face investigation and sanctions under the university’s academic misconduct procedures.

Education and Training for Responsible AI Use

Promoting ethical AI use requires not just rules but also education and awareness. Telkom University is committed to proactively teaching students and faculty about responsible AI practices:

  • AI Ethics Workshops and Seminars: The university will conduct regular workshops, seminars, and training sessions on “Academic Integrity in the AI Era” for both students and staff. These sessions (some in collaboration with the Center for Teaching & Learning and the IT office) will demonstrate practical scenarios of AI use and misuse. For example, workshops might show how to use AI to improve a draft without plagiarizing, how to cite AI-generated content properly, or how AI can produce false information. The aim is to help our academic community understand the capabilities and limitations of AI tools, fostering a culture of critical evaluation of AI outputs. Faculty will be trained on how to guide students and detect AI misuse, and students will be trained on how to integrate AI into their workflow ethically. Such educational efforts answer calls by experts who urge universities to update policies and actively disseminate guidance about AI to all stakeholders .

  • Orientation and Code of Conduct Updates: Starting from the new academic year, student orientation programs will include a module on AI literacy and integrity. New students will learn about this AI policy framework from day one, including real examples of what is allowed and what isn’t. The Student Code of Conduct and Academic Integrity Charter will be updated to explicitly mention AI tools – making it clear that the same standards of honesty apply to work involving AI assistance. Students may be asked to sign an honor statement acknowledging they will use AI responsibly and will not engage in AI-facilitated cheating or plagiarism. By formally embedding AI clauses into our conduct code, we leave no ambiguity that “academic dishonesty” encompasses misuse of AI as well. This preventive education is in line with global trends, where universities swiftly revise integrity policies to cover AI .

  • Guidelines and Tool-Specific Instructions: The university will publish user-friendly guidelines (in print and on the intranet) for popular AI tools like ChatGPT, including best practices and cautionary tips. For instance, guidelines will advise students how to prompt AI effectively for study help, how to double-check AI answers, and how to cite AI in their academic work if used. We will also highlight the terms of service and ethical use policies of these AI platforms, so users know, for example, that they shouldn’t share personal or sensitive data with them. If new tools (such as Google’s Gemini) emerge with different functionalities, the university will provide updated instructions on their appropriate use in the academic context. The idea is to treat AI tools similarly to other resources (like libraries or labs) – students should receive instruction on how to use them properly for learning.

  • Faculty Development and Policy Alignment: Faculty members will receive support and training to adapt their teaching and assessment in light of AI. The University’s Center for Teaching Excellence will hold sessions for instructors on designing AI-resilient assessments (e.g., oral exams, personalized writing assignments, project logs) and on incorporating AI in coursework beneficially (such as assignments where students must compare their work to an AI’s attempt or critique an AI-written piece to sharpen critical thinking). Instructors will also get access to resources on setting course-specific AI policies: e.g., sample syllabus statements that they can adopt, ranging from fully prohibiting AI to allowing it with attribution, depending on course goals . The university encourages a consistent yet flexible approach – consistent in upholding integrity, but flexible in allowing each discipline to decide how AI can be pedagogically utilized. All faculty are urged to explicitly communicate their AI usage expectations to students (in syllabi and in class), so students are not left guessing. This faculty engagement ensures that our policy is not just a document, but a practice lived in every classroom.

  • Student Resources and Support: We will empower students with resources to use AI responsibly. This includes library support (librarians can help students with citation of AI sources, or finding reliable information to cross-check AI outputs), writing center support (tutors can advise on integrating AI feedback without losing the student’s voice), and IT support (ensuring students have access to approved AI tools and understand data security). We may introduce an “AI Use Log” template that students can use to record how they employed AI in an assignment – this can be submitted with assignments as needed, and it serves both as documentation and a reflective tool for students to be mindful of their usage. Through these measures, students learn by doing – using AI under guidance and reflecting on their learning process.

Overall, the emphasis is on cultivating digital literacy and ethical habits around AI. Rather than simply forbidding AI (which is neither realistic nor beneficial to student development), Telkom University wants to produce graduates who are savvy in using AI and deeply grounded in academic integrity. Education and outreach initiatives will be continually refined based on feedback and evolving best practices, ensuring that our community stays informed about new developments (for example, if new detection tools or new AI capabilities arise, we’ll update our training accordingly).

Detection and Enforcement Mechanisms

Telkom University will employ a combination of technological and pedagogical measures to detect and deter the misuse of AI, coupled with fair procedures for enforcement:

  • AI Detection Software: The University will utilize reputable AI-content detection tools to scan submitted assignments for probable AI-generated text. For instance, our plagiarism detection system (e.g., Turnitin) has integrated capabilities to flag AI-written text with a certain confidence level. Additionally, tools like GPTZero and others may be used by instructors on a case-by-case basis to analyze suspicious submissions . It’s important to note that no AI detector is infallible – false positives and false negatives can occur. Thus, detection results will not be the sole basis for punishment; they will serve as indicators to prompt further review. If a student’s work is flagged by detectors, the instructor or an academic integrity committee may then manually scrutinize the work’s style, require the student to explain aspects of it, or compare it against the student’s past work to judge authenticity. These tools are continually improving, and Telkom University will stay updated on the latest detection technologies, using them in an ethically responsible manner (e.g., not subjecting students to unwarranted accusations based solely on an AI detector’s output).

  • Verification Methods: In cases of suspected AI misuse, instructors have the discretion to employ verification steps. A student might be asked to provide drafts, notes, or other evidence of their work process to show they did the work themselves. Or the instructor may conduct an oral follow-up: asking the student to verbally expand on or reproduce portions of the submitted work. If a student wrote a brilliant essay that is suspected to be AI-written, an oral discussion or on-the-spot writing exercise can quickly reveal whether the student possesses the knowledge and style reflected in the essay. Similarly, for coding assignments, an instructor might ask the student to walk through their code or solve a variant problem without AI. These methods serve both to confirm misconduct and to deter students from risking AI cheating (knowing they could be called to defend their work). The policy thus empowers faculty to take reasonable steps to authenticate student work when needed.

  • Enforcement Process: Suspected violations involving AI will be handled under the university’s existing academic integrity violation procedures. When a potential AI-related offense is identified, it will be reported to the Academic Integrity Committee (or relevant authority) for investigation. The student will be notified and given a chance to respond or present their case – due process is important for fairness. Evidence such as AI detector reports, the content in question, and any additional proof (e.g., identical AI-generated answers from another student, or the student’s inability to reproduce the work) will be considered. The committee will determine if a violation occurred, and if so, its severity (minor infraction vs. major cheating).

  • Proportional Disciplinary Actions: The policy prescribes a range of sanctions proportional to the nature of the violation, consistent with both university rules and Indonesian national guidelines. For minor first-time offenses (e.g. a student improperly quoted a small AI-generated passage without citation), the consequence might be remedial: such as mandatory academic integrity training and a warning, plus redoing the assignment properly or receiving a grade penalty on that work. For more serious or deliberate violations – such as using AI to write an entire paper or repeated offenses – stricter penalties apply. These can include a failing grade on the assignment or in the course, placement on academic probation, or suspension. In egregious cases (for instance, a student used AI to complete their entire thesis or committed fraud at scale), the university reserves the right to impose expulsion or revoke degrees, in line with national law that treats severe academic plagiarism as a serious offense . (Indonesia’s education regulations allow even for degree cancellation or legal action in cases of proven plagiarism, underscoring how serious academic fraud is taken .)

  • Fairness and Consistency: Enforcement of this AI policy will be carried out with fairness and consistency. All students will be held to the same standards; claims like “I didn’t know it was wrong to use ChatGPT” will not be considered a defense, given the extensive education efforts and clarity of this policy. However, if a student genuinely misunderstands an assignment’s AI policy and self-reports or clarifies immediately, the university may take that into account in mitigating penalties. Additionally, the university will consistently refine its approach as we learn from cases that arise. Any updates or clarifications to rules will be communicated widely. Importantly, the privacy and rights of students during investigations will be respected – e.g., a student’s work will not be publicly accused of being AI-generated without proper process, and any data (like AI detector results or drafts) will be handled confidentially.

  • Preventive Measures in Assessment Design: On the preventative side, Telkom University is encouraging instructors to design assessments that naturally discourage AI misuse. This includes in-class assignments, personalized project topics, oral presentations/defenses, and iterative writing processes where students submit outlines and drafts. By structuring courses to include these elements, opportunities for undetectable AI cheating are reduced. Students will know that it’s not just a single final product being graded, but their ability to discuss and process the material. Where feasible, courses might include spontaneous writing tasks or use proctoring for online tests to ensure authenticity. The policy supports faculty in these pedagogical choices by making it clear that requiring such steps (like an oral defense of a paper) is within their rights to uphold integrity. The combination of smart assessment design, student education, detection tools, and strict penalties forms a comprehensive enforcement approach.

In summary, Telkom University will be vigilant and serious in enforcing this policy. Students should be aware that misusing AI is neither undetectable nor excusable. Our message is reinforced by global developments – for example, many universities have adopted AI detectors and seen that even advanced language models can be identified with reasonable certainty in student work . While we celebrate the positive uses of AI, we equally commit to maintaining academic standards. Those who violate the trust of the academic community by cheating with AI will face consequences just as those who cheat in more traditional ways.

Alignment with National Standards and Global Best Practices

This AI policy framework is designed to be in harmony with Indonesia’s national educational standards and informed by practices at leading universities worldwide:

  • Compliance with Indonesian Academic Integrity Regulations: Indonesia has a strong legal and regulatory framework against academic dishonesty. The National Education System Act (No. 20 of 2003) and subsequent regulations (e.g., Ministry of Education Regulation No. 17 of 2010) explicitly prohibit plagiarism and mandate institutions to prevent and penalize it . These laws consider severe academic plagiarism a serious offense – historically even associating it with potential criminal sanctions to deter violators . Telkom University’s policy upholds the spirit of these regulations by treating AI-facilitated plagiarism and cheating with equal seriousness. In line with the 2010 directive’s holistic approach, we have outlined sanctions for students and faculty, and emphasized the role of university leadership in enforcement . Furthermore, our educational strategies fill a noted gap by focusing on proactive integrity training, which complements the national policy’s call for academic communities to build a culture of honesty. As Kemdikbudristek (Ministry of Education and Culture, Research and Technology) works on formulating national guidelines for AI in higher education , Telkom University’s framework will serve as a model of compliance and innovation, ready to adjust to any specific national policy once it is released.

  • Informed by UNESCO and International Guidelines: Our approach resonates with global recommendations, such as UNESCO’s 2023 guidance on the use of generative AI in education. UNESCO advocates a human-centered, ethical use of AI, calling for policies that support AI literacy, safeguard against misuse, and ensure AI augments rather than replaces education . Telkom University’s policy embodies these principles by encouraging critical thinking and requiring human oversight of AI outputs. We have also considered data privacy and equity concerns in line with international best practices (for example, ensuring AI use does not expose sensitive data, and that all students have equal access to tools if they are used). In Southeast Asia, Telkom University is among the pioneers addressing GenAI in academia, aligning with regional dialogues (such as the Southeast Asia Policy Dialogue on GenAI in higher ed hosted by Ditjen Dikti and UNESCO-ICHEI in 2024 ). By implementing this framework, Telkom University positions itself as a responsible, forward-looking institution in the global education community.

  • Benchmarks from Global Universities: In crafting these guidelines, we examined how peer institutions around the world are handling AI. Comparable global universities have begun updating their academic integrity policies to explicitly cover AI-generated content, and Telkom University follows suit. For instance, the University of California, Irvine’s policy now lists using work created by ChatGPT without citation as an example of plagiarism , and our definitions mirror this stance. Carnegie Mellon University and other top schools have issued syllabus guidelines that make clear unpermitted use of AI is an integrity violation, identical to traditional cheating . Harvard University’s provostial guidelines (July 2023) support responsible experimentation with AI while urging adherence to academic integrity and cautioning about accuracy and privacy – these points are echoed throughout our framework (we too emphasize careful verification of AI output and forbidding input of confidential data). Moreover, we took inspiration from universities that encourage AI literacy: for example, Florida Tech’s policy encourages using AI to enhance skills, not replace them , and we have built the same ethos into our principles. By referencing such best practices, we ensure our policy is on par with international standards and that Telkom University graduates remain competitive and ethically grounded in a global context. We have also considered differing approaches (some universities initially banned AI outright, others fully embraced it with guidelines); our policy strikes a balanced, middle path recommended by many academic experts – neither an outright ban nor uncritical permission, but a regulated, transparent use.

  • Continuous Improvement and Collaboration: Aligning with global best practices also means this policy is a living document. Telkom University will remain in conversation with international academic communities to update our approach as AI technology and norms evolve. We will participate in forums and share experiences with other universities, especially within Indonesia and ASEAN, to harmonize standards where appropriate. Should international standards (like publication guidelines, accreditation criteria, or new laws) emerge regarding AI usage, we will integrate those into our policy. In essence, this framework is both rooted in our national educational values and globally informed, ensuring that Telkom University maintains integrity while fostering innovation.

Implementation and Examples

Implementing this policy will involve coordinated efforts across the university. Below are some concrete steps and examples to illustrate how the policy would function in practice:

  • Policy Dissemination: The full policy will be published on the university website and academic handbook. Summary sheets highlighting do’s and don’ts of AI use will be distributed to students and faculty. For example, a one-page “AI Use Quick Guide” could be given to every student, listing acceptable vs. unacceptable uses in bullet points for easy reference, along with links to resources. Departments will discuss the policy in faculty meetings to ensure everyone interprets and applies it consistently.

  • Syllabus Integration: Starting immediately, every course syllabus should contain a statement about AI tool usage specific to that course. The Academic Affairs office can provide template language that instructors can adapt (ranging from “No AI tools allowed in any coursework” to “AI tools may be used for research of assignments but not writing the final submission, and must be credited”). For instance, a syllabus might say: “Students are permitted to use AI for preliminary research and grammar checking on essays, but all ideas and phrasing must be your own. Any direct use of AI-generated text is prohibited unless explicitly cited and approved by the instructor.” By front-loading these expectations, students will know from day one how the policy plays out in each class.

  • Example Scenario – Proper Use: A student is writing a report on cybersecurity trends. They use an AI assistant to gather a list of recent cyber attacks and to summarize some technical concepts. They then write the report in their own words, using the AI summary as a learning aid. In their bibliography or an acknowledgments section, they include a note: “ChatGPT was used to summarize background information on ransomware (prompt on 2025-03-01); the content was verified and written in my own wording.” They double-check all facts the AI gave them. This would be considered an acceptable use in line with the policy – the student learned from AI but did their own critical work and disclosed the help. As a result, the student’s work is original, accurate, and transparent.

  • Example Scenario – Violation: A student has a programming assignment due and decides to have an AI code assistant generate the entire solution. They copy-paste the AI’s code, maybe make a few minor edits, and submit it as their own. The instructor, noticing the code style doesn’t match the student’s past work, runs an AI code detection tool or simply asks the student to explain part of the code. The student cannot explain it well, and further analysis suggests it was AI-generated. This would be logged as a policy violation – unauthorized use of AI and misrepresentation. Per the policy, the student might receive a zero on the assignment and be referred for an integrity workshop. If it were a repeated offense or a large project, stiffer penalties would follow. This scenario underlines to students that it’s not worth the risk – instructors have ways to tell.

  • Ensuring Equitable Access: If instructors integrate AI tools as required parts of coursework (say, asking all students to use a specific AI platform for an assignment), the university will support this by ensuring all students can access the tool. For example, Telkom University might obtain a campus license for a certain AI software or ensure the free version suffices. If a tool is not freely available, instructors will be advised to provide alternatives or opt-in usage. This follows the principle that no student should be disadvantaged or forced into violating terms due to resource access issues . For instance, if a professor wants students to experiment with a paid AI design tool, the department could arrange a computer lab session where that tool is provided, or allow students who cannot use it to complete a similar task without the AI. Such measures ensure fairness and uphold the policy’s intent.

  • Monitoring and Review: The university will establish an AI Policy Review Committee consisting of faculty representatives from each school, IT staff, and student representatives. This committee will convene at least once per semester to review the implementation of the policy: analyzing any academic integrity cases involving AI, gathering feedback from teachers and students, and staying abreast of new AI developments. For example, if many students are confused about a particular aspect (say, how to cite AI or what level of AI editing is acceptable), the committee can issue clarifications or additional guidelines. If a new powerful AI tool emerges that changes the game, the committee can update the policy accordingly. An annual report on AI use and academic integrity could be prepared to inform the university community of how the norms are evolving. This dynamic approach ensures the policy remains relevant and effective over time.

  • Positive Reinforcement: The university will also celebrate and share examples of good practice where AI was used ethically to produce excellent work. For instance, if a student used an AI tool to help visualize data for a project and properly documented it, and the result was a deeper analysis, that could be showcased (with the student’s permission) as a model. By highlighting these success stories, we reinforce that using AI within the rules can enhance learning and achievement, motivating others to follow suit. It shifts the narrative from “AI as a cheating tool” to “AI as a learning enhancer when used right,” which supports the policy’s first objective of encouraging ethical AI use.

Conclusion

Telkom University’s academic policy on AI use establishes a clear, ethical, and pedagogically sound framework to navigate the rapid rise of artificial intelligence in education. We embrace innovation by encouraging our community to utilize AI as a collaborative assistant – a means to spur creativity, efficiency, and personalized learning. At the same time, we draw unmistakable lines against academic dishonesty: work submitted must be one’s own, with AI contributions openly acknowledged and only used as allowed. Through well-defined guidelines, continuous education, and robust enforcement, this policy safeguards the value of a Telkom University degree and the integrity of our scholarly endeavors.

This framework aligns with Indonesia’s academic standards and reflects global best practices, ensuring that Telkom University remains a leader in upholding academic integrity in the AI era. By implementing these policies and recommendations, we prepare our students to become responsible, ethical professionals who can leverage cutting-edge AI tools without compromising their honesty or skills. In essence, Telkom University is committed to producing graduates who are both tech-savvy and trustworthy – adept at working alongside AI, but never surrendering their own intellect or principles to it.
